Severity

P1
The lawyer is given something wrong or misleading and has no way to tell: a false “not found”, an unsupported in-force claim, a silently incomplete answer, a lost reply, or a dead end with no way forward.
P2
The answer is correct but incomplete, shallow or harder to verify, or the lawyer has to work around the tool to get what they asked for.
P3
Presentation, wording no lawyer has yet seen, or our own measurement and tooling.

Severity grades the defect, not the fix, and is a first-pass judgement from the transcripts and replays. Not to be confused with plan refs such as P1.1, which are rows in the fix plan.

Earlier, P3.2, an answer that changes under pushback, was measured before anything was built. In the session it comes from, the system took a position on how two defined terms in an assimilated EU regulation relate, defended it, then reversed it when the lawyer pushed back. Read against the regulation itself, that last reversal moved towards what the text says. So the original test, “hold your position unless you cite something new”, would have rewarded the wrong answer. The real problems are different. Its position changed three times in seven turns. It opened with “You are absolutely correct” before checking anything. And it stated as settled a reading the regulation contradicts.

The test, re-set before any fix. The system may change its position at most once, and only after checking the law again and citing what it found. It must make no claim the text contradicts and must not open by agreeing. It must still change its mind when the lawyer is right: two checks cover that, one in the same conversation and one in another session. Our reading of the regulation is to be confirmed by a lawyer.

How it was measured. We re-ran the conversation three times on the current system, and all three fail the new test. Two changed position three times. The third never changed at all: it held the contradicted reading through every challenge, so the system can be too stubborn as well as too pliable. In two of the three it also told the lawyer that part of the regulation was “not held” in the database, which is false.

One idea tried and dropped. We gave the system the exact text of the provisions the lawyer had named, including the passage that decides the question. In 12 tries it never cited that passage, and the position did not change once. The system has the text and argues past it. So the fix is about how it handles a point of interpretation, which is also P3.3's problem, and the two were taken together (above).

Earlier, P4.11, the wait when the model's provider stops responding mid-reply, was fixed. Sometimes the provider running the model stops sending a reply partway through and, after about two minutes of silence, drops it. The system used to try up to three times, so one such reply could add six to nine minutes to an answer. We re-sent 8 stored cases exactly as they were first sent, including the original date. They stalled again 19 times in 24, and a stalled request sent again unchanged stalled again every time (11 of 11). Changing only the date line made 7 of the 8 answer within seconds. The same cases also sometimes produced P4.10's runaway, so the stall looks like the same long thinking, cut off by the provider. Sending them to the model's other provider (Google AI Studio instead of Google Vertex) did not help: two stalled there too, and the other two ran away in full.

What changed. When a research step's reply stalls this way, it is no longer tried again. It goes straight to P4.5's “answer lost” label, and the chat asks for the research again, as a new request, which made good 3 of the 5 such cases in our replays. Across our replays this removes 10 wasted attempts, about 20 to 30 minutes of waiting in all. A reply refused because the provider was busy is still tried again (that works 13 times in 14), and so is a stalled chat reply.

How it was tested. What the model is sent does not change, only whether a stalled reply is tried again, so the test is a set of automated checks, one at each point the change touches. They fail if the fix is removed, and further checks fail if it reaches too far: if it stopped retrying a busy provider or the chat's own replies.

The cost we accept. In our replays, trying a stalled research step again rescued it once in 14 tries. That case now relies on the chat asking for the research again.

One row opened. P4.12: when the chat's own reply stalls, there is no second research request behind it (3 cases in our replays). Changing the date line suggests that trying again with slightly different wording might work where an exact repeat does not. It will be measured before anything is chosen.

One fix to our own measuring. Our tool for re-sending a stored research request rebuilt it with today's instructions. They have changed since most of these cases were recorded, so only 1 of the 8 was a faithful copy. The tool now rebuilds a request with the instructions of the day it was recorded, and all 8 match.

Earlier, P4.10, the cost of a lost reply, was fixed. Sometimes the model behind a research step thinks until it has used almost all the output it is allowed (about 63,000 tokens), then writes nothing. Each attempt took about six minutes and cost about 76 cents, and the system used to try three times. Across all our replays this happened 8 times, always in the quick research step behind a Conversational answer. Those 8 answers cost $12.38 more than the same questions normally do, and took nearly two hours longer in total. Trying again rarely helped (2 times in 12); the chat asking for the research again, which it already does, fixed all 6 cases where it was needed.

What changed. A research step's reply now has a ceiling of 32,000 tokens, and a reply that comes back empty after that much thinking is not tried again. It goes straight to P4.5's “answer lost” label, and the chat asks for the research again. A ceiling does not make the model answer, but it stops the waste sooner: in a test, a 16,000-token ceiling ended the same failure after 93 seconds and 20 cents. Telling the model to think less did not stop it at all.

How it was tested. Automated checks cover each part of the change, and fail if it is removed. On four normal stored questions the ceiling changed nothing. The last check, watching the ceiling stop a real runaway, was run on 25 September on the two stored cases. With the ceiling, each runaway ended at about 30,700 tokens, after about 165 seconds and 38 cents. Without it, the same runaway ran to about 63,000 tokens, 340 seconds and 77 cents.

Why that check could not be run at first. The research step's instructions begin with today's date. Given exactly the same text, the model gives much the same reply, so on a different day it was in effect being asked a slightly different question, and the runaway did not happen. Re-run with the date the question was first asked, the stored cases ran away again. Our re-run tools now keep the original date.

The cost we accept. Both runaways in that check would, without the ceiling, have ended in an answer after five to six minutes. With the ceiling they end with nothing: the step is labelled as lost (P4.5) and the chat asks for the research again, which in our replays made good every such case (6 of 6). That is still quicker and cheaper than waiting the runaway out.

Earlier, P4.5, a research step whose answer is lost, was fixed. About one question in sixty, the model returns an empty reply to a research step, even after three tries. Before, what that step handed on was only its record of the searches it had run, which reads like a search that found nothing, and in one Deep Research case the report told the lawyer “Step 1 found no results” about case law it had in fact retrieved. The system now detects the lost reply itself and labels the step as lost. The final report and the chat reply are told plainly that the step's answer was lost, not that nothing was found, and a warning at the top of the answer says so to the lawyer: “Research step 1 did not return its findings… This is not the step limit, and it is not a finding that the material does not exist.” The progress messages now say whether each step completed, stopped at the step limit or returned no reply, instead of “Step complete” for every step.

How it was tested. We counted every lost reply across all our replays first: 16 in 929 questions, 13 of them in a research step. In 15 of 18 lost steps the chat had already asked again and got an answer, so the warning is shown only when nothing made the loss good, or always for a Deep Research step. Automated checks cover each place the label must appear, and fail if the fix is removed. We then re-ran the answer-writing step on the stored cases, before and after. In the case that mattered, a lost step nothing made good, the reply before said, four times out of four, that the instrument was not held, with nothing to say the research had been lost. After the fix it disclosed the lost step every time.

One thing caught before release. The first wording made the chat reply blame “a lost reply from the database”, which is false and points the lawyer at the legislation database. The label now says the lost reply was the language model's own, and the re-run replies say so. The cost of a lost reply is a separate fix, P4.10, above.

Earlier, P4.7, a Deep Research report saying “no case law was found” when case law was never searched, is fixed. The step that writes the final report used one set of instructions for every research type, and its example of how to describe a gap was “No reported case law was found on X”, even under “Legislation only”. It now has instructions for each research type. They say what that type searched and what it did not, and they describe a gap by what happened (Thomas's wording). A source outside the research was not searched. A step that failed did not complete. Only a completed search can find nothing, and only in what it searched. The report's sections now follow the research type too: a “Legislation & case law” report has its own case-law section, and a Holyrood or Westminster report is no longer told to write a territorial-extent and in-force section.

How it was tested. Our replays and the pre-pilot answers held no instance of the sentence, so, as for P4.6, the test was chosen before building and run on the report-writing step alone. We re-ran that step on 28 stored Deep Research cases, before and after the fix. Before, one “Legislation only” report wrote exactly the sentence the review described (“…restricted by a Legislation Only filter, no reported case law was found…”), in two of four tries. After the fix, none did. Where case law had been searched and really found nothing, all six reports still say so, now as “the legislation and judgments retrieved in this research do not establish…”. Reports carry more links, not fewer, keep their subsection citations, and every one keeps its key-findings summary. One case showed the second half of the wording at work: a step that returned nothing, which the old report described as having “found no” case law, is now reported as a step that did not return findings. The Holyrood and Westminster sections are covered by automated checks only, because we have never re-run a parliament-bot session.

One thing caught before release. The first version of the new instructions made one report cite its sources as bare web addresses and another drop its key-findings heading. The same test caught both, the wording was tightened, and the test was run again in full.

Earlier, P3.7, a direct check of whether the database holds a named instrument, is fixed. When a question names an instrument by its number (for example SSI 2025/377), the system now looks that number up in the database before it starts researching. Before, it could only run a ranked keyword search, and not finding an instrument near the top of a ranked list is not proof that it is absent. The answer now says one of three things: the instrument is held; it is held but its text is not; or it is not held. When it is not held, a line under the answer says so, written by the system itself rather than left to the model: “SSI 2025/377 was looked up by its number and is not held in this index; that is a gap in the index, not a sign that the citation is wrong.” The official record of changes still says what an unheld instrument does, so the answer still reports that, for example, SSI 2025/377 brings section 18 of the Act into force.

How it was tested. We re-ran the two sessions where lawyers asked about instruments the database does not hold, three times each. Before the fix, none of 12 answers said definitely that the instrument was not held because it had been checked. After it, 11 of 12 did. The one that did not was a follow-up answered from the earlier conversation, which said the instrument’s text was not held; the line under that answer still states that the instrument itself is not held. That remaining wording is a new row, P3.15. The instrument that is held but has no text was reported correctly every time, no answer questioned the lawyer’s citation, and answers carry more links, not fewer.

Earlier, P4.6, wording that says the whole database lacks something, is fixed. Three lines in the research step's instructions told it what to say when its search came up short, and each said it about the whole database: “The available database does not contain information on this specific issue”, and a case-law equivalent. It now describes the search it actually made: “The material retrieved in this search does not establish the answer” (Thomas's wording). If a question needs a source it cannot search, such as case law in “Legislation only”, it says that source was not searched, not that nothing was found. No lawyer saw the old sentence in the pre-pilot, because it was only in Research mode, which was switched off. This fix is for when Research mode is switched on.

How it was tested. Its test was chosen again before the fix was built, from questions whose filter is known to have been “Legislation only”. Re-running those questions on the current system produced the sentence in none of 49 answers. Since P4.1, the system declines a case-law question under “Legislation only” itself instead of passing it to the research step, which is where most of these sentences came from. So we re-ran the research step directly on the 37 stored cases where it had written the sentence. Before the fix 32 of 32 reports carried it; after the fix 0 of 31 did. All 31 now say what was not searched, and the only two “database does not contain”-style sentences left are true: the legislation database holds no judgments. Answers carry more links, not fewer.
